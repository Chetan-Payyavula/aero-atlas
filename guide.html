F1 aerodynamics and regulations explained, 1961–2026

Common questions

What changes in the 2026 F1 regulations?

The 2026 cars are shorter, narrower and lighter. The maximum wheelbase drops by 200 mm to 3,400 mm and the width by 100 mm to 1,900 mm. The front wing is 100 mm narrower, tyres are 25 mm (front) and 30 mm (rear) narrower, and the FIA's minimum weight is 768 kg, though several teams quote 770 kg. Both wings now have movable flaps: Corner Mode keeps them closed for downforce and Straight Mode opens them to cut drag. Downforce falls by about 30% and drag by about 55%. The power unit loses the MGU-H, the MGU-K rises from 120 kW to 350 kW for a roughly 50/50 split between combustion and electric power, and the fuel is 100% sustainable.

What is DRS, and why is it gone in 2026?

DRS (Drag Reduction System) is a flap in the rear wing that a driver can open in set zones on the straights when within one second of the car ahead. Opening it cuts drag and adds top speed to help overtaking. It was introduced in 2011, the year after McLaren's driver-operated F-duct was banned. From 2026 every car opens both wings on the straights in Straight Mode, so DRS is replaced by Overtake Mode: extra electrical energy for a driver within one second of the car ahead.

What is ground effect in Formula 1?

Ground effect means using the car's underside as an upside-down wing. Air speeds up through the gap between the floor and the track, its pressure drops and the car is sucked down. Lotus started it with the 78 in 1977, sealing the sides of shaped sidepods with sliding skirts. Skirts were banned for 1981 and the flat-bottom rule ended it for 1983. It returned in 2022 with two long Venturi tunnels in the floor. In simulation, a car following one car length behind keeps 86% of its downforce, against 55% with the previous rules.

What is porpoising?

Porpoising is the bouncing that ground-effect cars can suffer at high speed. As the floor gets closer to the track it makes more downforce and is pulled lower, until the airflow under it stalls. The downforce then drops suddenly, the car rises, the flow reattaches and the cycle repeats. It hit many cars when ground effect returned in 2022; Mercedes' 'zeropod' W13 struggled with it and won only one race.

What was the Williams FW14B, and when was active suspension banned?

The FW14B was Williams' 1992 car. Its computer-controlled hydraulic active suspension held the ride height constant, so the floor and wings always worked at their best. It also had traction control and a semi-automatic gearbox. It won 10 races and both championships, with Nigel Mansell taking the drivers' title. Active suspension, traction control and ABS were all banned from 1994.

What was the Brawn double diffuser?

The 2009 rules cut back the diffuser at the back of the floor. Brawn GP found a loophole: an extra upper channel fed through a gap in the floor step, which gave it a second, 'double' diffuser. Rivals protested, it was ruled legal on appeal, and Brawn won both championships in its only season.

Why do F1 cars have a plank under the floor?

After the deaths of Roland Ratzenberger and Ayrton Senna at Imola in 1994, a 10 mm plank was fixed under every car to stop teams running them too close to the ground. Only 1 mm of wear is allowed, so a car run too low wears the plank and can be disqualified. For 1995 the floor also became stepped, which cut underbody downforce by up to 50%.

When did the halo become mandatory?

The halo, the titanium hoop that protects the driver's head, has been compulsory on every Formula 1 car since 2018.

How heavy is an F1 car?

The rules set a minimum weight, not a maximum. It was 450 kg in 1961, 500 kg in 1966 and 575 kg from 1973. Hybrid power units and safety structures pushed it up: 728 kg in 2017, 798 kg in 2023–24 and 800 kg in 2025. For 2026 it comes down to 768 kg (FIA figure; several teams quote 770 kg).

How wide is an F1 car?

The maximum width has changed several times. A width limit first appeared at the 1976 Spanish Grand Prix, where James Hunt's McLaren was disqualified for being 1.5 cm too wide, then reinstated. Cars were cut to 200 cm in 1993 and to 180 cm in 1998, widened back to 200 cm in 2017, and are 190 cm wide from 2026.

What are the MGU-K and the MGU-H?

They are the two motor-generators of the hybrid power units used since 2014. The MGU-K is connected to the crankshaft: it recovers energy under braking and gives it back as up to 120 kW of extra power (350 kW from 2026). The MGU-H is connected to the turbocharger and recovers energy from the exhaust. It is not part of the 2026 power units.

See it in the 3D model (2014) →

Why were high wings banned in 1969?

In 1968 teams began fitting wings on tall struts that pushed straight down on the wheel uprights. At the 1969 Spanish Grand Prix both works Lotuses crashed when their high wings failed. High wings were banned from the next race, at Monaco, and from then on wings had to be fixed to the sprung part of the car and could not move.

See it in the 3D model (1969) →

What were grooved tyres in F1?

From 1998 to 2008 slick tyres were banned to slow the cars. Tyres had 14 mm grooves, three on the front and four on the rear in 1998, and four on the front from 1999, leaving about 12% less rubber on the road. Slicks returned in 2009.

1961–1965 1.5-litre formula

The new 1.5-litre formula replaced the 2.5-litre cars. Engines sat behind the driver, bodies were slim cigar shapes, and nothing on the car made deliberate downforce. Lotus moved the chassis from a tube frame to a monocoque in 1962.

Key rules, 1961–1965
Engine1300–1500 cc, naturally aspirated only[11][10]
Forced inductionNot allowed[10]
Minimum weight450 kg[11][9]
FuelPump fuel (from 1963)[11]
Roll barRequired from 1963[11]
WingsNone used

Engines: 1.5-litre atmospheric engines, mostly V6 and V8. Ferrari's 1961 156 made about 190 hp at 9,500 rpm and weighed 460 kg; the 1965 Lotus 33 made about 215 hp at 10,800 rpm at 450 kg.[11]

Changes by year and landmark cars
  • 1961 1.5-litre formula starts; minimum weight 450 kg[11]
  • 1962 Lotus 25 brings the stressed aluminium monocoque
  • 1963 Pump fuel only, roll bar and double braking system required[11]
  • 1961 Ferrari 156: Shark-nose 120° V6 car that won the 1961 title with Phil Hill.
  • 1962 Lotus 25: First successful monocoque F1 chassis: a stiffer, lighter, slimmer tub than a tube frame.

1966–1967 Return to power

Engine capacity doubled to 3.0 litres. Bodies stayed cigar-shaped and wingless, but tyres grew wider to cope with the power. In 1967 the Lotus 49 bolted the Cosworth DFV straight to the monocoque so the engine carried the rear suspension loads.

Key rules, 1966–1967
Engine3000 cc atmospheric or 1500 cc supercharged[11][10]
Engine typesPiston, rotary or turbine allowed[10]
Minimum weight500 kg[11][9]
WingsNone used

Engines: 3.0-litre atmospheric (or 1.5 supercharged). The Cosworth DFV V8 arrived in 1967 and became the engine most teams bought for the next 15 years.[10]

Changes by year and landmark cars
  • 1966 3-litre formula, minimum weight 500 kg[11]
  • 1967 Lotus 49 and the Cosworth DFV as a stressed member
  • 1966 Brabham BT19: Repco V8 car; Jack Brabham won the title in a car carrying his own name.
  • 1967 Lotus 49: Engine as part of the structure; the DFV won on debut at Zandvoort.

1968–1969 Wings arrive

Teams turned aircraft wings upside down to push the tyres into the road. Rear wings were mounted on tall struts straight onto the suspension uprights. After both works Lotuses crashed when their high wings failed at the 1969 Spanish GP, the governing body banned high wings at Monaco and required wings to be fixed to the sprung part of the car.

Key rules, 1968–1969
Engine3000 cc atmospheric or 1500 cc supercharged[11]
Minimum weight500 kg[11]
Roll bar5 cm above the driver's helmet (1968)[11]
Wings from 1969Must be immobile and fixed to sprung parts only[11]
Bodywork 1969Height and width limits introduced (figures not given in these sources)[11][9]

Engines: Same 3-litre formula. Ford-Cosworth DFV, Ferrari V12, BRM V12, Matra V12.[10]

Changes by year and landmark cars
  • 1968 First rear wings at the Belgian GP; high strut wings spread
  • 1969 Spanish GP wing failures, high wings banned from Monaco[19]
  • 1969 Aero parts must be fixed and mounted to the sprung structure[11]
  • 1968 Lotus 49B: Tall strut wing acting directly on the rear uprights.
  • 1969 Matra MS80: 1969 title car; 360 hp at 9,000 rpm, 540 kg.

1970–1975 Wedges and sidepods

Radiators moved from the nose into sidepods beside the driver. That freed the nose to become a low wedge and pushed weight towards the middle of the car. Tall airboxes rammed air into the engine above the driver's head. Rear wings sat low on the gearbox.

Key rules, 1970–1975
Minimum weight530 kg (1970–71), 550 kg (1972), 575 kg (from 1973)[12][9]
Rear wing overhang≤ 100 cm behind rear axle (1973)[15]
Fuel tankBladder tanks 1970, crushable structure 1973[12]
Engine3000 cc atmospheric or 1500 cc supercharged; up to 12 cylinders[10]

Engines: 3-litre atmospheric, up to 12 cylinders from 1972. DFV V8s against Ferrari's flat-12.[10]

Changes by year and landmark cars
  • 1970 Lotus 72: wedge nose, side radiators, inboard brakes
  • 1972 Minimum weight 550 kg, safety foam in tanks[12]
  • 1973 Minimum weight 575 kg, deformable structure round the tank[12]
  • 1973 Rear wing overhang capped at 100 cm[15]
  • 1970 Lotus 72: Wedge body with side-mounted radiators: the start of sidepods.
  • 1975 Ferrari 312T: Transverse gearbox and flat-12; 580 kg.

1976 Airbox ban & width rule

From the 1976 Spanish GP the tall airboxes went and a maximum width came in. James Hunt's McLaren was disqualified at that race for being 1.5 cm too wide, then reinstated. Overhangs were tightened: front ≤ 120 cm, rear wing ≤ 80 cm behind the rear axle. Tyrrell ran six wheels to cut frontal area.

Key rules, 1976
Front overhang≤ 120 cm[15]
Rear wing overhang≤ 80 cm (was 100 cm)[15]
Overall widthLimit introduced 1 May 1976 (Hunt DSQ'd for 1.5 cm over)[16]
AirboxesTall airboxes banned[9]
Minimum weight575 kg[12]

Engines: 3-litre atmospheric. Air intakes moved to low NACA ducts or periscopes beside the roll hoop.[10]

Changes by year and landmark cars
  • 1976 New rules from the Spanish GP (1 May): width limit, airbox ban[16][9]
  • 1976 Front overhang 120 cm, rear 80 cm[15]
  • 1976 Tyrrell P34 six-wheeler wins in Sweden
  • 1976 Tyrrell P34: Four small front wheels to reduce frontal area and add front grip.
  • 1976 McLaren M23: DSQ'd at Jarama for being 1.5 cm too wide; reinstated in July.

1977–1982 Ground effect

Lotus shaped the underside of the sidepods like an upside-down wing and sealed the sides with skirts, so the whole car became a wing running in ground effect. Downforce jumped and cornering speeds with it. Brabham sucked air out from under its car with a fan. Skirts were banned for 1981 with a 6 cm ground clearance rule, but teams lowered cars on track to get round it.

Key rules, 1977–1982
Minimum weight575 kg (1980), 585 kg (1981), 580 kg (1982)[9][13]
Rear wing width110 cm (pre-1983 value)[15]
Rear wing overhang≤ 80 cm[15]
Skirts (1981)Banned, with mandatory 6 cm ground clearance[8][9]
Skirts (1982)Temporary reprieve[8]
Engine3000 cc atmospheric or 1500 cc turbo[10]

Engines: Renault brought the 1.5-litre turbo in 1977 (RS01). Atmospheric DFVs still won most races until the turbos became reliable.[10]

Changes by year and landmark cars
  • 1977 Lotus 78: shaped sidepod undersides with skirts[8]
  • 1977 Renault RS01: first turbo car in F1
  • 1978 Lotus 79 wins half the races; Brabham fan car withdrawn[8]
  • 1981 Skirts banned, 6 cm ground clearance; McLaren MP4/1 carbon tub[8]
  • 1982 Skirts briefly allowed again[8]
  • 1977 Lotus 78: First 'wing car': inverted-wing sidepods sealed by skirts.
  • 1978 Lotus 79: Refined Venturi tunnels and diffuser; won the title.
  • 1978 Brabham BT46B: Fan car: won the 1978 Swedish GP, then withdrawn.
  • 1981 McLaren MP4/1: First carbon-fibre composite monocoque.

1983–1988 Flat bottoms & turbos

Ground effect was cut off by rule: the floor between the tyres had to be flat and skirts were gone. Teams got the load back with huge rear wings and short dart-shaped sidepods. Turbo engines took over, then were throttled with boost and fuel limits.

Key rules, 1983–1988
FloorFlat between the inside of the tyres; no skirts[8][13]
Rear wingWidth 100 cm (from 110), overhang 60 cm (from 80), height up to 100 cm[15]
Minimum weight540 kg (1983–86), 500 kg (1987), 540 kg turbo (1988)[9][13]
Fuel per race220 L (1984–85), 195 L (1986–87), 150 L turbo (1988)[9][13] sources disagree
Turbo boost4.0 bar (1987), 2.5 bar (1988)[9][10][13]
RefuellingBanned from 1984[13]

Engines: 1.5-litre turbo V6 and four-cylinder engines. 1986 was turbo-only; 3.5-litre atmospheric engines returned as an option in 1987 while the turbos were choked by boost and fuel limits.[10][13]

Changes by year and landmark cars
  • 1983 Flat bottom, skirts banned; rear wing 100 cm wide, 60 cm overhang[8][15]
  • 1984 Refuelling banned, 220 L fuel limit[13][9]
  • 1986 Turbo engines only, 195 L[10][9]
  • 1987 Boost capped at 4.0 bar; 3.5 L atmospheric allowed[9][10]
  • 1988 Boost 2.5 bar; 150 L for turbo cars (one source gives 155 L)[9][13]
  • 1983 Brabham BT52: Dart-shaped flat-bottom car; first turbo-powered drivers' title (Piquet).
  • 1988 McLaren MP4/4: Honda turbo, low-line body; won 15 of 16 races.

1989–1993 Atmospheric 3.5 L & active cars

Turbos were banned. Noses rose from 1990 so air could reach the floor, and electronics took over: Williams' active suspension held the car at a constant ride height so the floor worked all the time. Cars got narrower and tyres smaller for 1993.

Rear wing height (1993)Lowered; sources give 95 cm or 90 cm[14][9] sources disagree

Engines: 3.5-litre atmospheric V8, V10 and V12. Honda V10/V12 and Renault V10 were the class of the field.[10]

1994–1997 Aids banned, plank & stepped floor

Active suspension, traction control and ABS were banned for 1994. After the deaths of Ratzenberger and Senna at Imola, downforce was cut mid-season and a 10 mm plank was fixed under every car to stop them running too low. For 1995 the floor became stepped and engines dropped to 3.0 litres.

Engine (1995)3.5 → 3.0 litres[7][14]
Overall width200 cm[6]

Engines: 3.0-litre atmospheric from 1995 (8–12 cylinders). V10s became the norm.[10][14]

  • 1997 Midship wings excluded by bodywork rules[14]
  • 1994 Benetton B194: Schumacher's first title, in the year active cars were banned.

1998–2008 Narrow track & grooves

Cars were narrowed by 20 cm and slick tyres were replaced with grooved tyres to cut grip. Aerodynamicists answered with bargeboards, turning vanes and more and more winglets. V10s gave way to 2.4-litre V8s in 2006.

Engines: 3.0-litre V10s mandated from 2000, revving past 19,000 rpm, then 2.4-litre 90° V8s from 2006 with a 19,000 rpm limit from 2007.[10]

2009–2013 Overtaking rules & slicks

A clean-sheet aero package meant to let cars follow: a wide, low front wing, a tall and narrow rear wing, no bargeboards or winglets, and slicks back. Brawn's double diffuser exploited a loophole and won the title. Refuelling was banned from 2010, so cars got about 22 cm longer, and DRS arrived in 2011.

Rear wing750 mm wide (from 1000), 150 mm higher: 950 mm (from 800)[5][9][15]
DiffuserMoved 330 mm rearwards[5]
BodyworkBarge boards, turning vanes and winglets banned[5]
TyresSlicks return; front 270 → 245 mm wide (2010)[5][9]
KERSOptional, 60 kW / 400 kJ per lap (~80 hp for ~6.5 s)[5][10]
Minimum weight642 kg (2013)[9]

Engines: 2.4-litre V8 at 18,000 rpm, eight engines per driver per season, plus optional KERS.[5][10]

Changes by year and landmark cars
  • 2009 New aero, slicks, KERS, 18,000 rpm[5]
  • 2010 Refuelling banned; cars about 22 cm longer[20]
  • 2011 DRS rear-wing flap mandated[20]
  • 2013 Minimum weight 642 kg[9]

2014–2016 Hybrid power units

The V8s were replaced by 1.6-litre turbocharged V6 hybrids with two motor-generators: the MGU-K on the crankshaft and the MGU-H on the turbo. Noses were forced low for safety and the front wing was narrowed.

Engines: 1.6-litre V6 turbo hybrid: internal combustion engine, turbocharger, MGU-K, MGU-H, battery and control electronics. Eight forward gears.[10][20]

2017–2021 Wider and faster

The rules turned towards more speed: cars 20 cm wider, much fatter tyres, a wider front wing, a lower and wider rear wing and a bigger diffuser. The halo became mandatory in 2018. Front wings were simplified and widened again in 2019.

2022–2025 Ground effect returns

For the first time since 1982, the floor is shaped into two long Venturi tunnels that make most of the downforce. Wings were simplified so the car leaves cleaner air behind it: in simulation a following car keeps 86% of its downforce one car length back, against 55% before. Wheels grew to 18 inches.

2026 Active aero & 50/50 power

Shorter, narrower and lighter cars with movable front and rear wing flaps. Corner Mode keeps the flaps closed for downforce; Straight Mode opens them on the straights to cut drag. Downforce is down about 30% and drag about 55%. The MGU-H is gone, the MGU-K nearly triples to 350 kW, and DRS is replaced by an Overtake Mode energy boost.

25 radical designs that read the rules differently

Cars where a team found something the rules did not forbid, from the first monocoque to the 2022 'zeropod'.

1962 Lotus 25

What the rules allowed
Nothing in the rules said how a chassis had to be built, and everyone used a welded tube frame.
What the team did
Made the chassis a stressed aluminium monocoque, so the outer skin carried the loads. It was stiffer, lighter and slimmer than a tube frame.
What happened
Jim Clark won the 1963 title in it. Within a few years every car had a monocoque.

What the rules allowed
